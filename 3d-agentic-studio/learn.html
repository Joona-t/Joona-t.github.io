Learn the basics

How a picture becomes a print

You don't need to know 3D printing or CAD to follow this. Here is the whole path in plain words, what the agent does and what you do, the real mistakes along the way, and the questions beginners ask.

The path

Six steps

  1. Idea or picture

    Start with an image. On this site many concepts were first drawn by an AI image generator; they are pictures of an idea, not files you can print.

  2. Printable drawing

    The picture is redrawn as clean shapes: black lines on white, like a stencil. Lines must be thicker than the printer's nozzle (0.4 mm here), so detail gets simplified.

  3. 3D shape

    A small program turns the drawing into a 3D model: a flat plate with the lines carved in or raised up, maybe in two colours.

  4. Slicer check

    The slicer (here Bambu Studio) cuts the model into layers, plans the printer's path, and estimates time and plastic. A clean slice means the printer can make it, not that it will look right.

  5. Print a test

    Print something small first: one bookmark, a detail sample, a test frame. Look closely at fine lines and how parts fit.

  6. Fix and repeat

    Change one thing and print again. The phone cases on this site went through six versions after the first one didn't fit.

Real examples

What went wrong, and what it taught

The phone case that didn't fit

The first TPU case sliced perfectly, then printed too tight. Its inside was 160.50 mm long; the phone is 160.71 mm. It was built on someone else's model, so we designed our own from Apple's published numbers instead.

Lesson: measure against the real object, and test the fit with the frame first. See our own case →

The slicer said no

A sculpted back carved into a one-piece case looked right in 3D. The slicer warned “floating regions”: printed face-down, the carved surfaces would start in mid-air. As a separate plate printed relief-up, the same shape needs no supports.

Lesson: read the slicer's warnings before printing; they are free test prints. See the two-part case →

Too big for the bed, once the tower is counted

A 240 mm plaque fits a 256 mm printer bed on paper, but a two-colour print also needs a “prime tower” to clean the nozzle. At 240, 230 and 227 mm they collided; 225 mm worked.

Lesson: leave room for more than the model. See the plaques →

Lines thinner than the nozzle

Hair strands and eyelashes in the concept art were finer than the 0.4 mm nozzle can draw. They had to be thickened or removed, so every printable drawing differs from its concept.

Lesson: compare the drawing with the idea before printing. See the bookmarks →

Where the agent comes in

What the agent does, and what you do

The agent

  • Writes the scripts that trace drawings and build 3D shapes
  • Prepares the printer project file
  • Runs the slicer and reads its report
  • Writes down what passed, what failed and why

You

  • Choose the picture and say what you want
  • Look at every result: does it still look like your idea?
  • Print the test and judge it with your own eyes
  • Decide what to change next

Agents make confident mistakes. Ask them to show evidence (a picture, a slicer report, a measurement), not just to say “done”.

Glossary

Words you will meet

Open the glossary (fourteen words)
Plaque
The flat square slab itself. Here 225 mm wide, 4 mm thick, flat on the back.
Stencil / engraving source
The black-and-white drawing that gets carved. Black is carved, white stays.
STL
A plain 3D shape file: just triangles. The viewer on this page loads these.
3MF
A project file for the printer software. It holds the shape, the two colours and the print settings together.
Slicer
The program that cuts a 3D shape into printable layers and predicts time and material. Here: Bambu Studio.
Layer height
How thick each printed layer is. 0.2 mm here, so a 4 mm plaque is 20 layers.
Filament
The plastic spool the printer melts. PLA is the common, easy kind.
Prime tower
A small sacrificial block printed next to the plaque so the colour change comes out clean. It needs room on the bed.
Watertight
A 3D shape with no holes or gaps, which printers need. The scripts check this.
TPU
A soft, bendy filament used for phone cases. Harder to print than PLA.
Two-colour print
One nozzle, two filaments: the printer pauses, swaps colour and carries on.
CAD
Computer-aided design: software for drawing objects in 3D. On this site the agent writes small programs that build the shapes instead, so you don't need to learn a CAD program first.
Agent
An AI assistant that can read files, write small programs and run them on your computer when you ask.
AMS
Bambu Lab's automatic material system. The AMS lite on an A1 holds four spools, so one print can use up to four colours. The 3D viewers on this site let you try that with the official colours.

Stencil reconstruction

Each concept is redrawn as a black-and-white engraving source at 1254 × 1254 px. Lines are thick enough for a 0.4 mm nozzle. Eight of the first ten were redone; the earlier versions are kept.

Image to outlines

The stencil is resized to 1100 px, thresholded (grey < 145 = ink), skeletonised, then thickened so every line is at least ~2.2 px ≈ 0.42 mm wide. Specks under 5 px are dropped and contours are traced.

build_plaque.py: skeletonize + distance ≤ 1.12 px

3D geometry

The outlines become an even-odd 2D cross-section scaled to the artwork area (plaque size − 16 mm). A 4 mm slab minus a 1.24 mm extrusion from z = 2.76 is the body; the same outline extruded 0.24 mm is the floor. Both are checked watertight.

manifold3d + trimesh · CC_n_geometry.json

Packaging the 3MF

Body and floor go into one 3MF object as two parts on extruders 1 and 2, placed at plate position (141, 128). Project settings come from an existing Bambu A1 template: PLA Matte on both slots, no supports.

package_plaque.py

Slicing and layer heights

Bambu Studio CLI slices each 3MF at 0.2 mm layers with a 0.4 mm nozzle: 20 layers for a 4 mm plaque. The red floor lands on one layer: the tool changes to T1 at layer 15 and back to T0 at layer 16.

batch_slice.py · slice-checks/CC_n/result.json · QUALITY_REPORT.json

The prime tower

Two colours on an A1 need a purge tower on the same 256 × 256 mm bed. At 240, 230 and 227 mm the plaque collided with it; 225 mm with a 20 mm tower at (2, 200) is the size that slices clean for all 25.

SHOWCASE.md · slice-checks/size-test-227, -230

Material profiles

All generated files use the Bambu PLA Matte @BBL A1 profile on both slots. The orange/yellow variants only swap the colour fields; they keep the same placeholder profile, so the translucent orange spool still needs its real profile set and a reslice.

Physical test prints

None are recorded in the project files. Slicing clean is not printing clean: fine details may merge, adhesion and the stand fit are untested. This site will show a sixth badge once a real print exists.

Questions

Beginner questions

Starting out

I don't code and I have never 3D-printed. Is this for me?

Yes. Read Learn first, look at the projects, then use a prompt from Make your own. The agent does the typing; you do the looking and deciding.

What is an “agent”, in plain words?

An AI assistant that can do more than chat: it can read files, write small programs and run them on your computer when you ask. Claude Code, Codex and Gemini CLI are examples. It will make mistakes, so ask it to show evidence.

Does it cost money?

The guide, the code and the prompts are free. Claude Code and Codex need a paid plan; Gemini CLI has a free tier. A bookmark uses a few grams of plastic; a 225 mm plaque about 150 g, around 5–6 hours of printing (slicer estimates).

What do I actually need?

An image, a computer with an agent, and access to a printer: your own, a friend's, a makerspace, a library or an online print service that accepts STL or 3MF files.

Can I use my own pictures, photos or logos?

That is the point. Use images you made or have the right to use. The fan art on this site stays here.

Where should I start?

With something flat and small, like a bookmark. Copy the “Just curious” prompt, give it to an agent with your image and read the answer. Nothing gets built until you say so.

Safety and honesty

Is 3D printing safe at home?

With care. The nozzle and bed get hot; keep fingers away while printing, and keep the printer away from children and pets. Print in a ventilated room. Printed PLA is not food-safe, and a TPU phone case has not been tested as drop protection. The Bambu Lab Wiki and Prusa Knowledge Base have safety guides.

Are the pictures on this site real?

The photos of prints are real. Pictures tagged “AI-generated concept” were made with an image generator to explore ideas. Renders of 3D models are pictures of the real files, not photos.

Why do some designs say “not printed yet”?

Because they haven't been. A clean slicer check means the printer can make it, not that it will come out well. Each design says exactly how far it got.

Can I sell prints of these designs?

Not the fan art: the characters belong to their rights holders. The code is MIT, so a version with your own designs is yours.

Can I download the phone cases?

Yes. The IH1 case is our own design, made from Apple's published phone dimensions, and free under CC BY 4.0. Print the frame first to check the fit; nothing has been printed yet. The older V1–V6 cases were built on another designer's model, so those files stay private.

Why isn't the phone case as deep as the concept picture?

Apple's drawing allows at most 2.1 mm of case on the back so MagSafe and wireless charging work. The concept looks several millimetres deep; ours fits its relief into 1.2 mm and relies on sharp edges and creases to catch the light.

Printing questions

What is a slicer?

The program that cuts a 3D model into thin layers and tells the printer where to move. Bambu Studio and PrusaSlicer are free slicers.

Why print a test first?

Because the first print of anything teaches you something: a fit that is too tight, lines that merge, colours that bleed. A small test costs minutes instead of hours.

How do two colours work with one nozzle?

The printer prints the first layers in one colour, pauses, swaps the filament, purges the old colour into a small waste tower, then carries on with the second colour.
